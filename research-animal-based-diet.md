# Animal-Based Diet Research: Paul Saladino's Approach

A practical guide for someone whose girlfriend is obese and wants to get healthy, with the constraint that she lives with her parents and can't control all meals.

Last updated: September 2026

---

## 1. What Is the Animal-Based Diet?

The animal-based diet is a dietary framework created by Dr. Paul Saladino (board-certified MD, former psychiatrist, author of *The Carnivore Code*). It sits between strict carnivore (zero plants) and standard whole-foods diets. The core idea: build every meal around animal foods for protein, fat, and micronutrients, then add back only the least toxic plant foods (fruit and honey) for carbohydrates.

**Three defining principles** (from Heart & Soil, Saladino's supplement company):
1. Prioritize well-raised meat, offal, and other animal products
2. Eat low-toxicity carbohydrates (fruit, honey) rather than grain/legume starches
3. Eliminate ultra-processed foods and seed oils

**Core foods (eat freely):**
- Red meat from ruminants: beef, lamb, bison, elk, venison (fatty cuts preferred)
- Organ meats: liver, heart, kidney, bone marrow, spleen, pancreas
- Eggs (pasture-raised preferred)
- Fruit: berries, bananas, mango, papaya, pineapple, dates, figs, citrus, watermelon
- Raw honey
- Raw dairy (if tolerated): raw milk, raw cheese, kefir, butter, ghee
- Animal fats: tallow, lard, duck fat, ghee, butter
- Bone broth
- Seafood (wild-caught): salmon, sardines, oysters, shrimp

**Medium toxicity (some people include, test individually):**
- White rice, sweet potato, squash, cassava
- Olive oil, coconut oil, avocado oil
- Coffee, tea
- Fermented vegetables (sauerkraut, pickles)
- Aged cheese (some tolerate better than fresh)

**Foods to avoid:**
- Seed oils (canola, soybean, sunflower, corn, grapeseed)
- Grains (wheat, oats, corn, most rice)
- Legumes (beans, lentils, peanuts, soy)
- Nuts and seeds (almonds, walnuts, chia, flax)
- Refined sugar and high-fructose corn syrup
- Ultra-processed foods (anything with an ingredient list you don't recognize)
- Most vegetables, especially: leafy greens (spinach, kale), cruciferous (broccoli, cauliflower, Brussels sprouts), nightshades (tomatoes, peppers, eggplant, potatoes)
- Artificial sweeteners

**The "plant toxicity" argument:** Saladino claims plants produce defense chemicals (oxalates, lectins, phytates, goitrogens) that can irritate the gut lining and block mineral absorption. Fruit is the exception because plants evolved it to be eaten for seed dispersal. This argument is debated and not settled science.

## 2. What Paul Saladino Actually Eats

Saladino has evolved his diet publicly over the years. In 2018 he started strict carnivore. By 2020 he added fruit and honey. By 2026 he's experimenting with starches (yuca works for him, white rice and sourdough don't).

**His daily template (2026, from his YouTube and blog):**

*Pre-surf (early morning):*
- Raw goat milk + frozen blueberries + grass-fed whey + raw honey + sea salt (smoothie)

*Second breakfast (~10 AM):*
- Cantaloupe or other seasonal fruit
- 5-6 oz grass-fed skirt steak
- Boiled yuca with sea salt

*Afternoon snack:*
- Raw Greek yogurt + banana + duck egg yolk + raw honey + meat sticks

*Dinner:*
- Grass-fed ground beef with honey and salt
- ~1 oz raw liver (a few nights per week)
- Glass of raw milk
- Yuca with grass-fed butter
- Watermelon
- Asparagus (he tolerates it, so he eats it)

**His principles (from his 2026 blog post):**
1. Single ingredient foods. If your great-grandmother wouldn't recognize the ingredients, it's not food.
2. High nutrients, low toxins, high digestibility.
3. Organs are non-negotiable. Whether fresh liver or desiccated supplements, get them in.
4. Match carbs to activity. More active = more carbs.
5. Buy the best food you can afford.
6. Stay curious, not dogmatic. Experiment and pay attention.

**Typical macros (varies by activity):**
- Protein: ~195g/day (25%)
- Carbs: ~320g/day from fruit, honey, and yuca (42%)
- Fat: ~118g/day (33%)
- He emphasizes that carbs do not make you obese or diabetic, and points to his fasting insulin of 3.1 and fasting glucose of 69 as evidence of insulin sensitivity despite high carb intake.

**Carb calculation from his website:**
Based on goal body weight and activity level:
- Low activity (0-3 hrs exercise): 0.7-0.9g per lb goal body weight
- Medium (3-6 hrs): 0.8-1.1g per lb
- High (6-12 hrs): 1.0-1.5g per lb
- Very High (12+ hrs): 1.2-1.7g per lb

## 3. How It Differs from Other Popular Diets

| Factor | Animal-Based | Carnivore | Keto | Paleo |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Defined by** | Food source (animal-first) | Food source (animal-only) | Carb limit (ketosis) | Ancestral philosophy |
| **Daily carbs** | 80-150g typical | ~0g | Under 20-50g | 15-30% of calories |
| **Fruit & honey** | Encouraged | No | Mostly avoided | Allowed |
| **Vegetables** | Mostly excluded | None | Low-carb veg encouraged | Allowed and encouraged |
| **Dairy** | Raw dairy preferred | Varies | Full-fat allowed | Excluded |
| **Nuts & seeds** | Avoided | No | Allowed | Allowed |
| **Seed oils** | Avoided | Avoided | Allowed (some versions) | Avoided |
| **Ketosis** | Not the goal | Naturally happens | The goal | Depends on carb intake |
| **Organ meats** | Strongly emphasized | Optional | Not emphasized | Not emphasized |
| **Eating to satiety** | Yes | Yes | Macro tracking common | Intuitive |
| **Calorie counting** | No | No | Sometimes | No |
| **Best for** | Nutrient density, autoimmune, long-term | Elimination protocol, autoimmune | Weight loss, blood sugar | Ancestral, moderate approach |

**Key differences in practice:**
- **Animal-based vs. carnivore:** Animal-based is more sustainable long-term because it includes carbs from fruit. Saladino quit strict carnivore after developing heart palpitations, cramping, and sleep disturbances that resolved when he reintroduced carbohydrates.
- **Animal-based vs. keto:** Keto allows avocado, olive oil, nuts, and low-carb vegetables but avoids fruit and honey. Animal-based does the reverse.
- **Animal-based vs. paleo:** Paleo includes vegetables, nuts, and seeds but excludes dairy and most animal organs. Animal-based excludes most vegetables and emphasizes organs.

## 4. Scientific Evidence

### What the research supports

**Higher protein diets improve body composition during weight loss.**
A combined analysis of two RCTs (MEASUR-UP and POWR-UP) found that a hypocaloric diet with higher protein (1.2 g/kg/day from lean red meat) promoted weight loss and improved physical function without adverse cardiometabolic effects compared to RDA-level protein (0.8 g/kg/day). Published in *Nutrients* (2022). [PMC9336183]

**Unprocessed red meat does not worsen weight maintenance or cardiovascular risk.**
An RCT of 108 adults found that diets containing 25g or 150g of unprocessed beef per day had similar effects on body weight, body composition, energy metabolism, and cardiovascular risk factors during weight loss maintenance. Published in *American Journal of Clinical Nutrition* (2022). [PMC9761757]

**Processed meat, not unprocessed meat, is the problem.**
The PREVIEW study (688 adults, 3-year follow-up) found that processed meat intake was associated with weight regain and worsened metabolic markers, but unprocessed red meat, poultry, dairy, and eggs showed no such association. Fish and seafood intake was inversely associated with triglycerides. Published in *Clinical Nutrition* (2022). [PMID: 35263691]

**Carbohydrate-restricted diets can improve metabolic markers.**
A meta-analysis of 174 RCTs (n=11,481) found that CRDs reduced body weight, waist circumference, fasting glucose, and triglycerides compared to higher-carb diets. Effects varied by replacement macronutrient. Published in *Nutrients* (2025). [PMC12799388]

### What the research does NOT support

**Long-term safety of carnivore/animal-based diets is unknown.**
A 2026 scoping review of carnivore diet studies (9 human studies included) concluded: "The CD may offer short-term health benefits but carries substantial risks of nutrient deficiencies, reduced intake of health-promoting phytochemicals, and the development of cardiovascular disease. At this time, long-term adherence to a CD cannot be recommended." The quality of evidence was rated "very limited" due to small samples, short durations, and no control groups. Published in *Nutrients* (2026). [MDPI 2072-6643/18/2/348]

**High LDL on low-carb diets is a real cardiovascular concern.**
The KETO-CTA study followed 100 lean metabolically healthy adults eating ketogenic diets with mean LDL-C of 272 mg/dL. After one year, they showed substantial coronary artery plaque progression. The study was later retracted due to methodological issues (unblinded image analysis, undisclosed conflicts), but the plaque progression was real. The follow-up paper was retracted in 2026. [JACC: Advances; CalibratedSignal analysis]

A clinical review from Amsterdam UMC described cases of severe hypercholesterolemia (LDL mimicking familial hypercholesterolemia levels) caused by high-fat, low-carb diets, including a case where LDL-C hit 7.9 mmol/L on a zero-carb diet and normalized after dietary modification. Published in *Nutrients* (2023). [MDPI Nutrients-15-00962]

**Plant-based diet quality is independently associated with lower mortality.**
A NHANES study (36,825 adults, 8.3-year follow-up) found that higher quality plant-based foods were associated with 34% lower all-cause mortality, while quality of animal foods showed no independent association with mortality. Published in *Journal of Nutrition* (2021). [PMC8547553]

### Saladino's LDL claims

Saladino has argued that high LDL cholesterol on a carnivore or ketogenic diet is not harmful in metabolically healthy people. This position is contradicted by the mainstream consensus. Mendelian randomization studies (654,000+ participants) show a linear relationship between LDL levels and cardiovascular disease risk. The National Lipid Association (2024) confirmed ApoB's superiority over LDL-C for risk assessment. Peter Attia and Tom Dayspring published a detailed critique in 2026 titled "There is no safe gamble with high LDL cholesterol."

A personal account from CalibratedSignal documented someone who followed keto/carnivore for 8 years with "clean labs" by carnivore community standards (low inflammation, good glucose) but developed a severe, flow-limiting coronary artery blockage by age 44. [calibratedsignal.com/articles/keto-trial-retracted-ldl]

**Bottom line:** The science supports replacing processed food with whole foods, eating adequate protein, and reducing ultra-processed items. It does NOT support the specific claim that high LDL is harmless on low-carb diets, or that all plant foods are harmful. The animal-based approach has merit as a whole-foods framework, but its more extreme claims (seed oils are poison, all vegetables are toxic, high LDL doesn't matter) are not backed by the weight of evidence.

Sources: [PMC9336183](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9336183/), [PMC9761757](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9761757/), [PMID: 35263691](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35263691/), [MDPI Nutrients 2026](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/18/2/348), [PMC8547553](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8547553/), [CalibratedSignal](https://calibratedsignal.com/articles/keto-trial-retracted-ldl), [Biolayne/Layne Norton analysis](https://biolayne.com/articles/research/paul-saladino-on-joe-rogan-experience-hype-vs-evidence/)

## 5. Practical Concerns

### Cost

The biggest misconception is that this diet requires expensive grass-fed ribeye every day. Here's what it actually costs on a budget:

**Budget animal-based day (under $10, US prices May 2026):**
- 3 eggs: $0.55
- 12 oz ground beef (conventional): $5.04
- 1 cup whole milk: $0.26
- 1 banana: $0.21
- 1 orange: $0.48
- 1 oz cheese: $0.30
- 1 cup yogurt: $1.00
- 2 tbsp honey: $0.55
- 1 sweet potato: $0.55
- 1 oz beef liver: $0.25
- **Total: ~$9.19/day (~$275/month)**

**Where to save:**
- Ground beef is the workhorse. Buy in bulk (5-10 lb tubes at Costco/Sam's Club for $3-4/lb)
- Beef liver is the cheapest cut at the butcher: $2-5/lb. It's the most nutrient-dense food per dollar
- Eggs are the best protein-per-dollar: $0.15-0.25 per egg
- Canned sardines: under $3 for omega-3s, calcium, B12, selenium
- Make your own bone broth from scraps
- Conventional meat is fine. Don't let perfect be the enemy of good

**Monthly range:** $200-400/month for one person, depending on cuts and quality. This is comparable to or less than a standard grocery budget when you factor in eliminated snack foods, processed items, and eating out less (because you're more satiated).

### Sustainability and social situations

**Living with parents who cook all meals:** This is the hardest practical constraint, and it's addressed in detail in Section 6 below.

**Eating out:** Easier than keto or carnivore. Order a steak or burger (no bun), ask for fruit instead of fries. Most restaurants can accommodate this without feeling restrictive.

**Social pressure:** Less restrictive than carnivore. You can eat fruit at gatherings, have honey in your tea, enjoy cheese. The hardest social situations are around grain-heavy meals (pasta night, pizza, sandwiches). Having a clear but non-preachy explanation helps: "I'm focusing on meat and fruit right now, I feel way better."

**Travel:** Bring desiccated organ supplements, beef jerky (sugar-free), and canned fish. Most places sell eggs and fruit.

### Cost of organ supplements

If she won't eat fresh organs (which is likely), desiccated organ capsules from Heart & Soil run about $40-60/month. This is not strictly necessary if she eats eggs and dairy regularly, but it fills micronutrient gaps.

## 6. Adapting for Someone Who Can't Control All Meals

This is the most important section. She lives with her parents. She can't choose what's cooked. She can't demand they stop using seed oils or stop serving rice. Here's what she can realistically do.

### Strategy: "Add, don't subtract"

Instead of trying to change the entire family kitchen (which will create conflict and resentment), she should focus on what she CAN control:

**Things she can control:**
- What she puts on her plate from what's available
- What she eats for breakfast (she likely makes this herself or it's simple)
- What snacks she buys for herself
- What she drinks
- Portions of the foods she should avoid
- Adding animal protein to family meals

**Practical actions, ranked by impact:**

**High impact (start here):**
1. **Eat more protein at every meal.** Whatever her parents cook, she can always add eggs, chicken, fish, or meat. If dinner is rice with curry, she takes extra curry and less rice. If there's a chicken dish, she takes two portions of chicken.
2. **Start the day with protein and fat.** Eggs cooked in butter with fruit. This sets her up for the day and reduces cravings for the processed stuff.
3. **Eliminate seed oils in her own cooking.** If she cooks any of her own meals, switch to butter, ghee, or tallow. Don't use canola, soybean, or sunflower oil.
4. **Stop drinking calories.** No soda, no juice from concentrate, no sweetened tea. Water, milk, or fresh-squeezed juice only.
5. **Replace snacks.** Instead of chips, cookies, or candy, snack on: hard-boiled eggs, cheese, fruit, yogurt, beef jerky, canned sardines.

**Medium impact:**
6. **Reduce rice and bread portions.** She doesn't have to eliminate them entirely, but cutting portions in half and filling the plate with meat and fruit makes a real difference.
7. **Ask for specific meals.** Instead of "I'm on a weird diet," she can say "Can we have more meat this week?" or "I'd love it if we could have eggs for dinner sometimes." Frame it as preferences, not rules.
8. **Buy her own protein staples.** Keep eggs, ground beef, cheese, and fruit stocked in the fridge. If her parents see her eating these, they may cook with them more.
9. **Skip the vegetable guilt.** If her parents serve vegetables she doesn't tolerate well (spinach, kale, broccoli), she can take a small portion and eat the meat and fruit instead. She doesn't need to make a scene about it.

**Lower impact but helpful:**
10. **Fruit as dessert.** Instead of whatever processed dessert is on offer, eat fruit. Ask her parents to keep fruit in the house.
11. **Read ingredient labels.** Learn to spot seed oils, high-fructose corn syrup, and artificial ingredients. Avoid products with long ingredient lists.
12. **Batch cook her own meals when possible.** If she has access to a kitchen, make ground beef patties, hard-boil eggs, and prep fruit for the week.

### What NOT to do:
- Don't try to change her parents' entire cooking style. That creates family conflict and makes her the "difficult" one.
- Don't go 100% carnivore or animal-based overnight. That's unrealistic when someone else cooks.
- Don't skip meals because they're "not compliant." Eating something is better than eating nothing.
- Don't make food a source of stress or shame. The goal is health, not perfection.

### A realistic "animal-based in a family kitchen" day:

**Breakfast (she controls this):** 3 eggs scrambled in butter + a banana + glass of milk
**Lunch (family meal):** Double portion of whatever meat is served + small portion of rice + skip the vegetables she doesn't tolerate
**Dinner (family meal):** Focus on the protein component, reduce starch portions, have fruit for dessert
**Snacks:** Hard-boiled eggs, cheese, yogurt, fruit, beef jerky

She won't be hitting Paul Saladino's exact macros. That's fine. She'll be eating more protein, less processed food, fewer seed oils, and more whole foods than before. That alone will produce results.

## 7. Common Criticisms and Counter-Arguments

### Criticism 1: "Cutting out all vegetables is unhealthy"

**The criticism:** Vegetables provide fiber, vitamins, phytochemicals, and antioxidants. Major health organizations recommend 5+ servings daily. Studies consistently link vegetable consumption to lower mortality.

**Saladino's counter:** He argues that organ meats and fruit cover the micronutrient gaps, that fiber is not essential (many cultures thrive on low-fiber diets), and that plant defense chemicals cause more harm than the benefits justify.

**The honest take:** He has a point about organ meats being nutrient-dense. He's wrong to dismiss all vegetables. But the practical reality for someone coming from a standard diet is that replacing chips and cookies with meat and fruit is a massive upgrade, even if she never eats kale. The dose makes the poison. Small amounts of well-cooked vegetables are fine for most people.

### Criticism 2: "This diet will raise your cholesterol and cause heart disease"

**The criticism:** High saturated fat intake raises LDL cholesterol. Mendelian randomization studies show LDL is causally linked to atherosclerosis. The KETO-CTA study showed plaque progression in lean people on keto with very high LDL.

**Saladino's counter:** He claims high LDL is not dangerous in metabolically healthy people and that particle size matters more than total LDL.

**The honest take:** The cardiovascular risk concern is real and well-documented. For someone who is obese, losing weight will likely improve their lipid profile regardless of diet. But she should get bloodwork done before and after, and track LDL/ApoB. If LDL climbs above 160 mg/dL, that's a conversation to have with a doctor.

### Criticism 3: "There's no long-term research on this diet"

**The criticism:** Animal-based eating as a named framework is only a few years old. The longest carnivore studies are observational surveys, not controlled trials. We don't know what happens over decades.

**The honest take:** This is fair. But the same criticism applies to keto, paleo, and many other dietary approaches. What we DO have is thousands of years of human populations eating nose-to-tail animal foods (Inuit, Maasai, various indigenous groups). The modern animal-based diet is essentially a modified version of what many traditional cultures ate.

### Criticism 4: "This is just an expensive way to eat meat"

**The criticism:** Grass-fed beef, raw dairy, and organ supplements are costly. Most people can't afford this.

**The honest take:** As shown in Section 5, budget animal-based eating costs under $10/day. Beef liver is the cheapest cut at the butcher. Eggs are the most affordable protein source on earth. The diet can be done on a tight budget if you prioritize ground beef and eggs over ribeye.

### Criticism 5: "Paul Saladino changed his story. He quit carnivore."

**The criticism:** He wrote a whole book about carnivore being optimal, then started eating fruit. Doesn't that undermine his credibility?

**The honest take:** Actually, it's the opposite. He noticed his health deteriorating on strict carnivore (palpitations, cramps, sleep issues) and changed his approach. That's intellectual honesty. Most diet gurus double down forever. He listened to his body and adapted. You can disagree with his framework, but the willingness to evolve is a positive.

### Criticism 6: "Eliminating fiber is bad for gut health"

**The criticism:** Dietary fiber feeds beneficial gut bacteria. Low-fiber diets are associated with reduced microbiome diversity.

**The honest take:** This is partially valid. However, many people with IBS, SIBO, or gut inflammation actually improve on low-fiber diets because fermentable fibers feed the wrong bacteria. The carnivore/animal-based approach works well as a temporary elimination protocol for gut issues. Long-term, some people reintroduce well-tolerated fiber sources (fruit, squash) successfully.

## 8. Getting Started Protocol

### Phase 1: Preparation (Week 0)

Before changing anything:
1. **Get baseline bloodwork.** Lipid panel (total, LDL, HDL, triglycerides, ApoB), fasting glucose, fasting insulin, HbA1c, comprehensive metabolic panel. This gives her a starting point.
2. **Take a "before" photo and measurements.** Weight, waist circumference. Photos are more motivating than scale numbers.
3. **Stock the kitchen.** Buy: eggs, ground beef (80/20), butter, fruit (bananas, berries, whatever she likes), yogurt, cheese, honey, salt.
4. **Accept imperfection.** She won't be eating like Paul Saladino. She'll be eating like someone who controls 30-50% of her food choices and is making them count.

### Phase 2: The first two weeks (Elimination of the worst offenders)

**Goal:** Remove the most harmful foods while keeping it realistic.

**Do eliminate:**
- Seed oils (cook with butter instead)
- Ultra-processed snacks (chips, candy, cookies, packaged meals)
- Sugary drinks (soda, juice from concentrate, sweetened tea)
- White bread and pastries

**Do increase:**
- Eggs (3-4 per day if she likes them)
- Ground beef or any red meat
- Fruit
- Cheese and yogurt
- Butter/ghee for cooking

**What to expect:**
- Days 3-5: Possible fatigue, headaches, carb cravings. This is normal. Salt food generously, drink water, eat more fat.
- Week 1-2: Cravings diminish, energy stabilizes, reduced bloating.

### Phase 3: Weeks 3-4 (Building the template)

**Goal:** Find her animal-based template that works within family meals.

- She should be eating protein at every meal
- Fruit replaces processed snacks and desserts
- Rice/bread portions are smaller
- She's cooking some of her own meals (eggs, ground beef patties)

### Phase 4: Month 2+ (Optimization)

**Goal:** Refine based on how she feels.

- Consider adding organ meats (start with liver: 2-3 oz per week, mix into ground beef if she doesn't like the taste)
- Get follow-up bloodwork at 3 months
- Track what makes her feel good and what doesn't
- Adjust carb intake based on activity level

### Supplements to consider (not required, but helpful):
- **Electrolytes** during the first 2 weeks (sodium, potassium, magnesium)
- **Vitamin D** if she doesn't get much sun
- **Magnesium glycinate** before bed (helps with sleep and cramps)
- **Desiccated organ capsules** if she won't eat fresh organs (optional)

### Red flags that mean she should stop or see a doctor:
- Persistent dizziness, fainting, or heart palpitations
- Hair loss (could indicate calorie restriction or thyroid issues)
- Severe constipation lasting more than 2 weeks
- Worsening bloodwork (LDL above 200, fasting glucose rising)
- Disordered eating thoughts (restrictive diets can trigger this)
- Feeling miserable after 4+ weeks (the diet isn't for everyone)

## Summary: What Actually Matters

The animal-based diet framework, stripped of its most extreme claims, boils down to:

1. **Eat real food.** Meat, eggs, fruit, dairy. Stop eating ultra-processed junk.
2. **Eat enough protein.** This is the single most important change for weight loss and metabolic health. Protein is satiating, builds muscle, and raises metabolic rate.
3. **Cut seed oils and refined sugar.** These are the two most harmful components of the modern diet, and virtually all dietary frameworks agree on this.
4. **Don't fear fruit or honey.** They're whole foods with fiber, vitamins, and minerals. Compared to a candy bar, they're nutritionally superior.
5. **Organ meats are optional but excellent.** If she'll eat them, great. If not, eggs and dairy fill most gaps.
6. **Perfection is the enemy.** She doesn't need to follow Paul Saladino's exact template. She needs to eat more protein, less processed food, and make the best choices available given her living situation.

For someone who is obese and living with parents who cook, the highest-impact changes are:
- **Eat double the meat, half the rice** at family meals
- **Start every day with eggs and fruit**
- **Stop drinking sugar**
- **Replace snacks with protein and fruit**

That alone, sustained for 3-6 months, will produce meaningful weight loss and health improvement. Everything else is optimization.
